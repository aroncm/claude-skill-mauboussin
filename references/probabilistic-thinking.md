# Probabilistic Thinking and Base Rates

## The Core Philosophy

Michael Mauboussin emphasizes thinking in probabilities and distributions rather than certainties and point estimates. This approach acknowledges uncertainty and leads to better decisions.

> "Life is about probabilities, not certainties. The goal is to assess these probabilities as accurately as possible and bet accordingly."

## Base Rate Thinking

### What Are Base Rates?

**Base rates** (also called **outside view** or **reference class**) are the statistical frequencies of outcomes in situations similar to the one you're analyzing.

**Example**:
- Inside view: "Our new restaurant has a great location and talented chef"
- Outside view (base rate): "70% of restaurants fail within 5 years"

### Why Base Rates Matter

1. **Counteract Optimism Bias**: We naturally focus on unique features while ignoring statistics
2. **Improve Accuracy**: Base rates provide empirical foundation
3. **Calibrate Expectations**: Anchor on what typically happens
4. **Avoid Overconfidence**: Recognition that your case may not be that special

### The Outside View vs Inside View

**Inside View**:
- Focus on specifics of the case
- Detailed scenario construction
- Emphasis on unique features
- Often overly optimistic or pessimistic

**Outside View**:
- Look at statistics of similar situations
- Use relevant reference class
- Consider frequency of outcomes
- More accurate for prediction

**Best Practice**: Start with base rate (outside view), then adjust for specifics (inside view) with justified reasons.

## Applying Base Rates to Investing

### Corporate Performance Base Rates

**Earnings Growth**:
- Base rate: Most companies see earnings revert toward GDP growth (3-5%)
- High growth (20%+) rarely sustained beyond 5-7 years
- Adjust expectations accordingly for growth stocks

**Return on Equity/Capital**:
- Base rate: ROE tends to regress toward cost of equity
- Exceptional returns (>15% ROIC) typically mean-revert over 10 years
- Only widest moats sustain high returns long-term

**Market Share Stability**:
- Leaders often maintain position (70% probability in stable industries)
- Challengers rarely become leaders (< 20% success rate)
- Industry matters significantly (tech more dynamic than utilities)

### M&A Base Rates

**Acquisition Success**:
- Base rate: 60-70% of acquisitions fail to create value for acquirer
- Large premium deals (>40%) even worse odds
- Stock deals worse than cash deals
- Related acquisitions better than unrelated

**Implications**:
- Skepticism warranted on acquisition announcements
- Track record matters more than specific deal story
- Price paid is critical variable

### IPO and New Company Base Rates

**IPO Performance**:
- First day pop often followed by underperformance
- Base rate: IPOs underperform market over 3-5 years
- Exception: Profitable companies in favorable markets

**Startup Success**:
- Base rate: ~90% of startups fail
- ~10% become sustainable businesses
- <1% become "unicorns" (>$1B valuation)

**Implications**:
- Extreme selectivity required
- Portfolio approach necessary
- Most value in tiny number of winners

### Turnaround Base Rates

**Corporate Turnarounds**:
- Base rate: 20-30% of turnarounds succeed
- CEO change improves odds slightly
- Financial distress very hard to escape
- Industry tailwinds matter more than heroic efforts

**Implications**:
- Skeptical of turnaround stories
- Wait for evidence of inflection
- Margin of safety essential

## Probability Distributions, Not Point Estimates

### The Flaw of Single Forecasts

Traditional approach:
- "I think this stock will earn $5.00 per share"
- "This company will grow 15% per year"

Problems:
1. Implies false precision
2. Ignores uncertainty
3. Doesn't reflect range of outcomes
4. Leads to overconfidence

### Think in Distributions

Better approach:
- "I think EPS has 50% chance of $4-6, with tails at $2-8"
- "Growth likely 10-20%, with 80% confidence"

Benefits:
1. Acknowledges uncertainty
2. Captures range of possibilities
3. Enables probabilistic analysis
4. Improves calibration over time

### Constructing Probability Distributions

**Scenario-Based Approach**:

1. **Identify key uncertain variables** (revenue growth, margins, etc.)

2. **Build scenarios**:
   - Optimistic (10-20% probability)
   - Base (50-60% probability)
   - Pessimistic (20-30% probability)
   - Extreme outcomes (5-10% probability each tail)

3. **Estimate outcomes for each scenario**

4. **Calculate probability-weighted expected value**

**Example: Company Valuation**

| Scenario | Probability | Outcome | EV |
|----------|-------------|---------|-----|
| Strong growth | 20% | $150 | $30 |
| Base case | 50% | $100 | $50 |
| Weak growth | 25% | $70 | $17.50 |
| Distress | 5% | $20 | $1 |
| **Total** | **100%** | | **$98.50** |

If current price is $80, expected return = 23%

### Fat Tails and Extreme Outcomes

**Don't Ignore the Tails**:
- Low probability extreme outcomes can dominate expected value
- "1% chance of 100x" matters more than "50% chance of 2x"
- Both positive and negative tails important

**Power Law Distributions**:
- Venture capital: a few big winners drive all returns
- Corporate longevity: few companies last decades
- Market drawdowns: occasional massive declines

**Implications**:
- Portfolio construction matters
- Position sizing based on tail risks
- Asymmetric bets can be valuable (limited downside, unlimited upside)

## Expected Value Decision Making

### The Formula

**Expected Value = Σ (Probability × Outcome)**

### Simple Example

Bet: Pay $10, flip coin, heads = $25, tails = $0

Expected Value = (0.5 × $25) + (0.5 × $0) - $10 = $12.50 - $10 = **+$2.50**

Positive expected value → Take the bet

### Investing Application

Don't ask: "Will this stock go up?"

Ask: "What is the probability-weighted expected return?"

**Example Analysis**:
- 60% probability: +20% return
- 30% probability: -10% return
- 10% probability: -50% return

Expected return = (0.6 × 20%) + (0.3 × -10%) + (0.1 × -50%) = **+3%**

Now compare to alternatives and risk tolerance.

## Process vs. Outcome

### Key Insight

> "A good process can lead to a bad outcome, and a bad process can lead to a good outcome. Judge the process, not just the outcome."

### The 2x2 Matrix

| | Good Outcome | Bad Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Good Process** | Deserved success | Bad luck |
| **Bad Process** | Good luck | Deserved failure |

### Why This Matters

**Problems with judging only outcomes**:
1. Luck plays a role in outcomes
2. Good/bad luck can persist for periods
3. Creates wrong incentives
4. Teaches wrong lessons

**Benefits of focusing on process**:
1. Process is controllable, outcomes partly random
2. Good process wins over time
3. Enables learning even from bad outcomes
4. Reduces hindsight bias

### Application to Investment Analysis

**Good Investment Process Includes**:
- Thinking probabilistically
- Using base rates
- Seeking disconfirming evidence
- Quantifying key assumptions
- Proper position sizing
- Having a sell discipline
- Learning from mistakes

**Poor Investment Process**:
- Confirmation bias
- Ignoring base rates
- Overconfidence in precision
- Concentration without conviction
- No sell discipline
- Not learning from outcomes

**Key Point**: You can lose money with good process (bad luck) and make money with bad process (good luck). Over time, process wins.

## Calibration and Improving Probabilistic Thinking

### Calibration

**Definition**: How well your stated probabilities match actual frequencies

**Example**:
- If you say "80% confident" you should be right ~80% of the time
- Most people are poorly calibrated (overconfident)

### How to Improve Calibration

1. **Keep a decision journal**:
   - Record predictions with probabilities
   - Track outcomes
   - Review and learn

2. **Use ranges, not points**:
   - Give 80% confidence intervals
   - Track how often actual falls in range

3. **Decompose complex estimates**:
   - Break into components
   - Estimate each separately
   - Combine (reduces overconfidence)

4. **Seek feedback**:
   - Compare predictions to outcomes
   - Learn where you're over/under confident

5. **Use tools**:
   - Prediction markets
   - Reference classes
   - Statistical models

### Common Calibration Errors

**Overconfidence**:
- Ranges too narrow
- Tail probabilities too low
- "Sure things" that aren't

**Underconfidence**:
- Less common, but happens
- Usually in areas of expertise

**Solutions**:
- Widen your ranges
- Think about what could go wrong
- Use base rates to anchor

## Practical Investment Framework

### Before Making an Investment

1. **What's the base rate for this type of situation?**
   - Similar companies historically
   - Industry patterns
   - Relevant reference class

2. **What probability distribution of outcomes is reasonable?**
   - Best case scenario and probability
   - Base case scenario and probability
   - Worst case scenario and probability
   - Expected value

3. **How is this case different from the base rate?**
   - Specific reasons for deviation
   - Evidence for your view
   - Could you be wrong?

4. **What's the expected value versus alternatives?**
   - Risk-adjusted returns
   - Opportunity cost
   - Portfolio impact

5. **What would change your mind?**
   - Indicators to monitor
   - Milestones for thesis
   - Exit criteria

### Portfolio Management

**Diversification**:
- Reduce impact of being wrong on any one bet
- Enable more aggressive individual positions
- Balance correlations

**Position Sizing**:
- Size based on expected value AND uncertainty
- Larger positions when high confidence, good odds
- Smaller positions for uncertain but attractive expected value
- Use Kelly Criterion as guide (not formula)

**Kelly Criterion (simplified)**:
Position Size = (Expected Return) / (Variance of Returns)

### Continuous Learning

1. **Track your predictions**: Journal with probabilities
2. **Analyze your misses**: Where were you wrong and why?
3. **Study the outcomes**: What was luck vs. process?
4. **Refine your approach**: Update based on feedback
5. **Stay humble**: Markets are complex, surprises happen

## Key Probabilistic Thinking Principles

1. **Think in probabilities, not certainties**
2. **Start with base rates, adjust with specific information**
3. **Use probability distributions, not point estimates**
4. **Focus on expected value, not just possibility**
5. **Judge process, not just outcomes**
6. **Track predictions to improve calibration**
7. **Consider tail outcomes, not just central cases**
8. **Acknowledge what you don't know**
9. **Update beliefs with new information (Bayesian thinking)**
10. **Embrace uncertainty as reality, not weakness**

## Avoiding Common Mental Traps

**Narrative Fallacy**: Don't let compelling story override base rates

**Confirmation Bias**: Seek disconfirming evidence actively

**Hindsight Bias**: "I knew it all along" prevents learning

**Availability Bias**: Recent/vivid events feel more probable

**Anchoring**: Don't get stuck on irrelevant numbers

**Overconfidence**: Most people think they're above average

**Solution**: Structured, probabilistic thinking + humility
